The harbor town wakes before the sun does. By half past four the bakery on the corner has its lights on, and the smell of bread drifts down the slope toward the water, where the first boats are already sliding out between the breakwaters. Nobody in town remembers who built the breakwaters. They are older than the church and older than the road that connects the town to the rest of the coast, and the stones are worn so smooth that children dare each other to walk along the top of them at low tide.

In spring the hills behind the town turn a green so bright it looks painted. Farmers bring early greens and eggs to the square on Saturday mornings, and the market runs until the last crate is sold or the rain arrives, whichever comes first. An old woman who sells honey has kept the same spot beside the fountain for thirty years. She labels every jar by hand with the name of the flower the bees visited, and she will tell anyone who asks that the clover honey is for tea and the heather honey is for bread, and that mixing them up is a mistake you only make once.

The school sits halfway up the hill, a long low building with tall windows that catch the afternoon light. Its library has more books about ships than about anything else, because for most of the town's history that was what people needed to know. There are logbooks donated by retired captains, tide tables going back a century, and a shelf of novels about shipwrecks that every student reads at least once and pretends not to have enjoyed. The teacher who runs the library keeps a map of the coast on the wall and marks each wreck with a small red pin.

Summer brings visitors. They arrive on the afternoon train with cameras and folded maps, and they walk slowly along the harbor wall, stopping to photograph the nets drying in the sun. The cafes put tables out on the pavement, and the evenings stretch long and golden. On the longest day of the year the town holds a festival with music in the square, lanterns strung between the lampposts, and a race in rowing boats from the lighthouse to the fish market. The winning crew is given a barrel of cider, which they are expected to share with everyone who cheered for them, and they usually do.

The lighthouse itself has not had a keeper for decades. The light runs on its own now, turning through the night with a steady patience, and the keeper's cottage has become a small museum. Inside there are brass instruments, oil lamps, a logbook open to a winter night when a storm kept the keeper awake for three days, and a narrow staircase that visitors are allowed to climb on clear afternoons. From the top you can see the whole curve of the bay, the islands to the south, and on a very clear day the faint line of the mainland mountains far to the east.

Autumn is the season the town likes best, though few people would say so aloud. The visitors go home, the light softens, and the orchards on the hills fill with apples. Families spend weekends picking fruit and pressing it, and for a few weeks every kitchen smells of cinnamon and cooked sugar. The fishing changes too: the boats go out farther and stay out longer, and the catch comes in silver and heavy in the evening. Down at the market the auctioneer talks so fast that only the regular buyers can follow him, and newcomers stand at the back pretending to understand.

Winter is hard and quiet. Storms roll in from the west and the harbor fills with boats that cannot go out. The men and women who fish mend nets in the sheds along the quay, and the sound of their radios carries across the water: weather reports, old songs, the slow voice of a man reading the shipping forecast. The bakery opens later. The school closes for a week when the road floods. People visit each other in the afternoons, bringing soup or a loaf of bread, and the conversations wander from the weather to old stories and back to the weather again.

There is a story everyone in town knows, about a winter long ago when the harbor froze for the first time in living memory. The ice was thick enough to walk on, and for three days the whole town walked out onto it, children and grandparents alike, to look back at their houses from a place no one had ever stood before. Someone brought a fiddle. Someone else brought chestnuts and a small iron stove. When the thaw came the ice broke apart with a sound like distant thunder, and the boats were back in the water within a week. The story changes a little every time it is told, but the fiddle and the chestnuts are always in it.

The town has changed in other ways. The cannery closed and became a workshop for boat builders, then a gallery, then a workshop again. The railway station got a new roof. A young couple opened a bookshop in the old customs house and fill its windows with whatever they are reading that month. The church bell was recast after it cracked, and some people say it rings a half tone higher than it used to, though no one can prove it. Through all of it the bakery has kept its hours, the honey seller has kept her place by the fountain, and the light at the end of the breakwater has kept turning.

Evenings in town end the same way in every season. The last ferry comes in, its wake spreading in slow lines across the harbor. The streetlamps flicker on one by one along the quay. Someone closes the shutters of the cafe, someone walks a dog along the harbor wall, and the gulls settle on the roofs of the fish market. Out past the breakwater the water goes dark, and the lighthouse begins its long patient count through the night, the same count it has kept for longer than anyone in the town has been alive.
